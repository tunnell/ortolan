Ortolan

Generate auditable 256-bit keys using dice (or coins) — no electronics, no software RNG, no trust required until you type it in

Why physical randomness? Digital random number generators have trust problems. The NSA backdoored Dual_EC_DRBG for seven years. Debian's OpenSSL generated breakable keys for 20 months. Intel's RDRAND is unauditable silicon. Many devices — especially embedded systems, hardware wallets, and air-gapped machines — can't guarantee quality randomness at all.

The fix: trade randomness from mathematics on a deterministic system for something in the physical world. Dice under a blanket. You can see every roll. No firmware, no supply chain — while you generate, nothing to trust but physics.

Why "Ortolan"? The ortolan bunting is a small bird traditionally eaten under a napkin. This tool recommends generating your password under a blanket for visual privacy. The bird is now protected by the EU — please don't eat them.

What This Is For

Keys that face offline attack and live for years: full-disk encryption, a password manager's master password, a wallet seed. For those, eliminating RNG trust is worth half an hour, and 256 bits — NIST's recommendation for long-term sensitive data — holds about 128 against Grover's quantum algorithm. It is the wrong tool for website logins: rate-limited services don't reward this much entropy, and some will truncate or reject passwords of this length (50–150 characters, depending on mode). The assumed attacker can backdoor RNGs, watch and listen from a distance, and seize your hardware later — but does not already control the machine you will type into. If they do, no password scheme survives; see phase 4.

Phase 2 is the only row whose every entry is in front of your eyes: generation is observable and checkable by hand, which is the property no digital RNG can offer. A third party still can't verify your rolls — only you, watching them, can — so this card says auditable, not verifiably random. The other rows are where your trust actually goes; the security boundary is all six of them (Ellison 2007), and the card walks them in order.

Why Not a Computer?

Every digital random number generator you might use instead — /dev/urandom, Intel's RDRAND instruction, a hardware wallet's onboard TRNG — is a black box. You cannot observe or verify the entropy source. History shows this trust has been catastrophically misplaced:

Notable digital RNG failures
Dual_EC_DRBG (2006–2013) — A NIST-standardized PRNG contained a kleptographic backdoor. NSA spent ~$250M/year on its Sigint Enabling Project to influence commercial product designs, and reportedly paid RSA Security $10M to make Dual_EC_DRBG the default in BSAFE. Withdrawn from NIST SP 800-90A after the Snowden disclosures.

Debian OpenSSL (2006–2008) — A well-intentioned code change to suppress a Valgrind warning removed two lines from OpenSSL's md_rand.c, reducing entropy from 128+ bits to ~15 bits (the process ID). Every OpenSSL-generated SSL/SSH key on Debian-based systems for 20 months was trivially breakable. CVE-2008-0166.

Intel RDRAND — Processes raw entropy through on-chip AES-CBC-MAC whitening, making output fundamentally unauditable. Linux kernel developer Theodore Ts'o resisted pressure to rely on RDRAND alone. The CrossTalk vulnerability (CVE-2020-0543) later showed RDRAND outputs leaked across CPU cores via a shared staging buffer.

Milk Sad (2023) — Libbitcoin Explorer seeded a Mersenne Twister with 32 bits of clock time, collapsing 2256 keyspaces to 232; roughly $900k was swept in coordinated thefts (CVE-2023-39910). Trust Wallet shipped the same pattern (CVE-2023-31290), exploited in the wild. The lesson cuts both ways: Milk Sad was a key-generation tool. Perfect physical entropy does not protect you from the software that later consumes it — see phase 4.

Dice from a board game and the coin in your pocket have none of this. You can watch every roll and every flip, and the physics are well understood (see the notes in each tab). There is nothing inside them to trust.

1 · Prepare the Room

Before you start — one sweep of the room
  • Every microphone and radio out of the room, powered off — not muted, not airplane mode: phone, watch, earbuds, laptop, smart speaker. A phone mic recovers over 90% of keystrokes (Harrison 2023), and an active MEMS microphone leaks its audio as RF whatever the software says (Onishi 2025).
  • No calls while rolling or while typing. Keystroke recovery works at 93% over Zoom with no language model, and at 91.7% on random keys over VoIP — "my password is random" is not a defense here.
  • Work silently: don't murmur characters as you write or type. A vibrating window or light bulb can carry room audio out even after the devices are gone (Lamphone 2022).
  • Webcam covered. No screen will ever display the key (phase 4).
  • Watch and fitness band off your writing wrist.
  • Write on one loose sheet on a clipboard — not on a pad (impressions), not on the bare desk (vibrations).
  • Read the printed tables once: every cell distinct, the grid complete. A corrupted table discards entropy silently, and this is the one integrity check a human can do in a minute.
  • If you want to fairness-test your dice, do it another day — the tester runs on a device this checklist just removed.
  • Assume every room can be bugged, including yours. The best you can do is an empty room with every device gone; away from home, prefer waiting until you're back.
  • Blanket over you and the workspace. It stops line of sight and nothing else — not sound, not desk vibration, not RF, not the heat your fingers will leave on the keys; the lines above and phase 4 cover those. It looks silly. It works.

For the rolling itself: no published attack recovers die faces or coin outcomes from sound or vibration — an absence of research, not a guarantee. Either way, the microphone sweep in the first line already covers it.

Design — Words

A passphrase instead of a character string: 20 words drawn from the EFF large wordlist, each selected by five dice rolls read as a number from 11111 to 66666. Since 65 = 7,776, every five-roll group maps to exactly one word with zero waste — 12.9 bits per word, 258.5 bits for 20. Words are the default because paper and fingers survive them: a misread character inside dragster is obvious and recoverable, where one in a random string is fatal (Chatterjee 2016). The costs are length — about 150 characters with separators — and paper: the full list is embedded at the bottom of this tab and prints with it, about a dozen pages. The list is public; only your rolls are secret.

Roll one die five times in order, or five distinguishable dice read in a fixed order. Never sort or rearrange dice by value — reading them in size order collapses 12.9 bits per word to 8.0.

Join words with a single space. The separator carries no entropy, but the exact string is the password — pick one and keep it forever. (No word in the list begins another, so even no separator is unambiguous.)

Procedure — Words (phases 2 and 3)

  1. Roll a die five times, writing each digit as it lands, left to right (e.g., 24136).
  2. Find the five-digit number in the wordlist and write the word in the Word column.
  3. Repeat steps 1–2 for 20 words. Generation ends here; the key now exists on paper. (A die misread as it lands costs nothing — whatever you wrote is the key, and it's just as random. Errors after the written record are the ones that lock you out.)
  4. Verify: cover the Word column, redo every lookup from the digit columns, and write the result in the Check column. Where the two disagree, the lookup was wrong — fix it from the raw digits. Proofreading by eye misses errors; an independent second entry is the method that actually works (Barchard 2011).
  5. Transcribe the Word column into both record grids below, one space between words.
  6. Read each record back against the worksheet, word by word, pointing with a pencil.

Design — Coin

The alphabet is 32 characters: the digits 0–9 plus 22 lowercase letters, excluding i, l, o, u (Crockford's Base32). The exclusions make handwriting self-correcting: anything that reads as i/l/1 is 1, anything that reads as o/0 is 0. Keep 5/s and 2/z distinct when writing. Since 25 = 32, every group of 5 flips maps to exactly one character with zero waste. The final character uses a 1-bit table. A literal . is inserted every 5 characters for readability — dots carry no entropy but can be left in the password.

Procedure — Coin (phases 2 and 3)

  1. Shake the coin between cupped hands — don't look at which face is up — flip, and catch it in your palm; never let it spin or bounce. Do this 5 times, writing the bits left to right (e.g., HTTHT → 10010).
  2. Look up the 5-bit string in Table A and write the character in the Char column.
  3. Repeat steps 1–2 for 51 groups (255 flips, characters 1–51), then flip once more and look up the single bit in Table B for the 52nd character. Generation ends here; the key now exists on paper. (A flip misread as it lands costs nothing — whatever you wrote is the key, and it's just as random. Errors after the written record are the ones that lock you out.)
  4. Verify: cover the Char column, redo every lookup from the flip columns, and write the result in the Check column. Where the two disagree, the lookup was wrong — fix it from the raw bits. Proofreading by eye misses errors; an independent second entry is the method that actually works (Barchard 2011).
  5. Transcribe the Char column into both record grids below, inserting a . every 5 characters.
  6. Read each record back against the worksheet, character by character, pointing with a pencil.

If a stored copy fails years from now, remember the alphabet has no i, l, o, or u — read those as 1, 1, 0, 0 — and try swapping 5/s and 2/z before declaring the key lost (Chatterjee 2016).

Coin Security Notes

Coin bias: 50.8% same-side — negligible for key generation.  Diaconis, Holmes & Montgomery ("Dynamical Bias in the Coin Toss," SIAM Review 49(2), 2007) showed that human thumb-flips cause the coin to precess (wobble), spending slightly more flight time with the starting face up. They predicted P(same side) ≈ 0.51. Bartoš et al. confirmed this empirically with 350,757 flips by 48 people using 46 different currencies ("Fair coins tend to land on the same side they started," arXiv:2310.04153, 2023; JASA 2025): measured P(same side) = 0.508, 95% CI [0.506, 0.509], while P(heads) = 0.500, 95% CI [0.498, 0.502] — no coin denomination or country showed a heads-vs-tails bias when flipped. The cost of a 0.508 same-side bias, if you take no countermeasure: 0.047 Shannon bits over 256 flips — or about 5.9 bits in the min-entropy accounting NIST SP 800-90B applies to key material, leaving ~250 bits either way. An unusually wobbly flipper (P(same side) = 0.54, 2σ in Bartoš's data) loses ~28 bits, leaving ~228 — still ~114-bit security even against Grover. Or make the whole question moot: Bartoš et al.'s own recommendation is to conceal or randomize the coin's starting side, which takes the measured bias to exactly zero — which is what step 1 of the procedure does.
Always FLIP, never SPIN.  Spinning a coin on a table introduces large, coin-specific biases driven by physical asymmetry. Spinning a US penny on a table lands tails roughly 55% of the time. The largest dataset, 20,422 spins by students of Robin Lock at St. Lawrence University (Chance News 11.02, 2002), measured 45.0% heads. Gelman & Nolan (The American Statistician 56(4), 2002) discuss the physics of why spinning introduces such biases, including the widely reported finding by Polish researchers Gliszczyński & Zawadowski that the Belgian €1 coin landed heads ~56% when spun. These are entropy-destroying biases. Flip the coin with your thumb and catch it in your hand. Never let it spin or bounce on a surface. And don't look at which side is up before you flip — the same-side bias is a memory of the starting face; deny yourself the memory and it's gone, at zero flip cost.
Von Neumann's extractor — for bent coins, not wobbly thumbs.  Von Neumann (1951): flip twice; HT → heads, TH → tails, HH/TT → discard and reflip. Because P(HT) = p(1−p) = P(TH) for any fixed bias p, this removes any bias in the coin itself — a bent coin, a weighted coin — at a cost of ~4 flips per fair bit (budget ~1,024 flips for 256 bits; Peres 1992 improves the efficiency).

What it cannot remove is the same-side bias, which is not a lopsided coin but a correlation between consecutive flips: von Neumann's guarantee assumes independent flips, and its output inherits whatever correlation the input had (Blum 1986 gives the Markov-robust extension). Since flipped coins show no fixed bias at all (Bartoš et al.), the fix that matters is randomizing the starting side — step 1 of the procedure. Use von Neumann only if you distrust the coin as an object; the Sia coins-in-a-cup method (see Prior Art) is robust to per-coin bias in a way von Neumann is not.

Dice Security Notes

Dice bias — negligible and unknowable to attackers.  Store-bought dice have minor imperfections: recessed pips remove more material from the 6-face than the 1-face, shifting the center of mass toward the 1-face (making 6 slightly more likely to land face-up). Campbell & Dolan (Western Kentucky University, 2019) found that detecting whether a die is unfair requires ~3,000 rolls — and even then, most commodity d6 dice tested fair.

An attacker doesn't know which dice you used or their specific bias. Each die's imperfections are unique. Averaged across the global population of dice, biases cancel out. Unlike a backdoored RNG where the attacker knows the flaw, dice bias is local, small, and varies randomly between dice. Treat that as a bonus on top of the conservative accounting below, not a substitute for it.
No debiasing needed.  Unlike coins (where the same-side bias is systematic across all coins), dice bias is idiosyncratic and tiny. A ~1–2% bias on any individual face, over 50 rolls, costs about 3 bits of min-entropy — the conservative measure NIST SP 800-90B uses for key material — leaving ~255.6 of the 258.5. Von Neumann-style debiasing would waste rolls for no practical security gain.
Whose dice?  An attacker cannot realistically tamper with the world's supply of commodity dice — d6s are made by hundreds of companies and sold in every toy store. A targeted attacker can tamper with yours: dice that arrived as a gift belong to a different threat model than dice from your own Monopoly box. Use dice you already owned, or transparent precision dice. A grossly loaded die fails the fairness tester within about a hundred rolls, and even the worst case — one face at 50% — leaves ~100 min-entropy bits: below the quantum margin, but still beyond classical brute force behind a real KDF. Tampering degrades the scheme and gets caught; it doesn't quietly break it.
Rolling technique matters.  Dice must bounce and tumble — don't drop flat or slide. Roll onto a hard surface with walls (dice tray, box lid, book). A dice tower provides consistent randomization. Roll both dice simultaneously; read left-to-right or use different colors to distinguish first vs. second die.

4 · Enter

Everything up to this point trusted nothing but physics and your own eyes. Typing ends that. From here the keyboard, the operating system, and the receiving software are trusted components, exactly as they are for any password — physical entropy buys nothing against a keylogger, and this is the one trust dice cannot remove. It is not hypothetical: KeePass, named below as a consumer of this password, leaked typed master passwords out of process memory (CVE-2023-32784), and derived keys linger in RAM, swap, and hibernation files long after entry (Halderman 2008). Enter the key on the cleanest machine the secret deserves: full-disk encryption on, hibernation off or swap encrypted, powered down when you finish. An air-gapped or auditable-I/O device (e.g., a Precursor) shrinks this phase's trusted base the way paper shrank generation's.

Screens and cameras.  Shoulder surfing works: in NYU's IllusionPIN study, 21 of 21 unprotected attacks succeeded, and a phone camera with 5× zoom extends the range dramatically. Newer work needs no view of the keyboard at all: upper-body motion in a video call leaks keystrokes (Sabra 2021), and on-screen text reconstructs from its reflection in the typist's eyeglasses (Private Eye 2023). Type the key blind into a masked field; never display it on a screen. Touch-type it — hunt-and-peck is the typing style both the acoustic and thermal papers single out. Keep the blanket on for entry, webcam covered, no calls (phase 1). It still looks silly. It still works.
Electromagnetic emanations (TEMPEST).  Vuagnoux & Pasini (EPFL/LASEC, USENIX Security 2009) demonstrated recovery of 95% of keystrokes from PS/2 keyboards at up to 20 meters through walls. All 12 keyboard models tested were vulnerable. USB keyboards were also found vulnerable, though PS/2 keyboards were easiest to attack. Van Eck phreaking of CRT monitors was demonstrated at hundreds of meters in 1985. Even Faraday cages can be defeated: Guri et al.'s ODINI attack (IEEE TIFS, 2020) exfiltrated data through Faraday walls via low-frequency magnetic field modulation. The channel is not historical: the same emanation class now reads modern HDMI and mobile screens (Screen Gleaning 2021; Deep-TEMPEST 2024).

Implication: When entering your password into a device, do so in an interior room away from external walls and windows. Ideally use a device with a verified, auditable I/O path (e.g., the Precursor's FPGA-based architecture, where the entire CPU is instantiated from open RTL source). The blanket (phase 1) stops line of sight, not EM.
Heat.  The keys you pressed stay warm: non-experts recover full short passwords from a thermal image up to 30 seconds after entry, partial input to 60 (Thermanator 2018), and AI-assisted recovery reaches 86% at 20 seconds (ThermoSecure 2023). Accuracy falls with password length — at these lengths the keyboard saturates and the image mostly reveals which keys you touched — so the real thermal target is the short PIN or login password guarding the same machine later. The mitigation is free: swipe both palms across the whole keyboard after entry, or wait a minute before leaving it unattended.
Key derivation.  If using this password as input to software (not as a raw key), ensure the software applies a proper KDF (PBKDF2, Argon2, or HKDF). Standard in VeraCrypt, LUKS, KeePass, etc. The KDF stretches your high-entropy input and binds it to a salt, producing the actual symmetric key. It is also the first software your key ever touches — which is why this phase began by naming the receiving software a trusted component.

5 · Prove It Works

Before anything is destroyed, prove the key round-trips. Create the encrypted volume, wallet, or database; close it; reopen it by typing the password fresh from the copy you intend to keep — not from muscle memory of the last five minutes. The software's "confirm password" prompt proves nothing here: if you misread the worksheet once, you will misread it the same way twice. The round trip is the last moment an error is recoverable, because the worksheet still exists. When it passes, and only then, go to phase 6.

6 · Keep and Destroy

Keep: the record.  Two fates for two kinds of paper. The record — the sheets with the finished password — is the thing you keep. You will not reliably memorize this key, words or characters: a 56-bit secret took study participants a median of 36 practice sessions, and 6% never managed it (Bonneau & Schechter 2014). If the paper is lost, the key is lost, and surveys of self-custodied keys consistently find loss, not theft, is the dominant failure (Krombholz 2016). Keep the two verified copies in separate places. Paper begins to char at about 180 °C and house fires reach about 700 °C, so the copy that must survive one belongs in a UL 72 Class 350 document safe or stamped into a metal plate. For high-value keys, splitting the secret across custodians (SLIP-0039, codex32) is an option — with the honest caveat that its usability for non-experts is largely untested. Every extra copy is one more thing to steal; that tension is real, and where to settle it is your call.
Destroy: everything under the record.  Worksheets, and the sheets that lay beneath them, are what you destroy — after phase 5 has passed and not before. Forensic electrostatic detection (ESDA, first commercialized in 1978 by Foster & Freeman) can recover pen indentations from up to 6 sheets beneath the page you wrote on. Seward's charge transport model (J. Forensic Sciences, 1999) explains the mechanism: paper-to-paper friction creates differential surface charges detectable by ESDA's corona wire. Under normal storage conditions, impressions can persist for decades.

Best practice: Write on a single loose sheet on a hard clipboard, the clipboard on a rug or towel (phase 1). If you used a notepad, tear out and destroy the first 7 pages beneath the writing sheet. Oblique lighting reveals impressions 3–4 pages deep; ESDA reaches up to 6, hence the 7-page margin.

Destruction method: Cross-cut shredding (DIN 66399 level P-4) is minimum. For high-security: wet pulp the pages (soak until fibers separate, then blend) or burn to ash. NSA guidance warns that even books thrown into a fire can remain half-readable after an hour. Strip-cut shredding has been reconstructed — most famously by Iranian students during the 1979 hostage crisis. Laser printers also stamp every page with tracking dots identifying the printer and time (Richter et al. 2018, DEDA), so destroying the worksheet covers attribution as well as content — or rule the worksheet by hand on blank paper and the printer never enters the ceremony.

Prior Art

Physical randomness for key generation is well-established. Arnold Reinhold's Diceware (1995) uses five dice to generate passphrases from a 7,776-word list (~12.9 bits/word); Antonov & Georgieva later gave Diceware-style generation its academic security analysis. The EFF published improved wordlists in 2016 (Joseph Bonneau). In the Bitcoin ecosystem, the Glacier Protocol (unmaintained since ~2020), ColdCard, BlueWallet, and BitBox02 all support manual entropy input via dice or coins, and David Vorick's "coins in a cup" method (Sia Foundation, 2020) uses modular arithmetic on five coins for entropy-preserving debiasing. Post-2020, dice entry went mainstream in hardware signers (SeedSigner, Keystone) — with documented user losses from under-rolling, and an in-firmware dice-to-seed conversion the user cannot check. Ortolan's fixed-length worksheet makes under-rolling structurally impossible, and the paper bijection is the conversion step, checkable by eye.
